# Deep research request

## Framing question

I want to substantiate (or nuance) a distinction I observe experientially: the difference between **metabolic breathlessness** (adaptive hyperventilation matching real O₂ demand — you breathe a lot but don't *feel short of air*) and **functional breathlessness** (a disproportionate sensation of air hunger on modest objective effort, driven by breathing-pattern dysregulation of emotional/postural origin — you don't breathe well and *feel short of air* despite low metabolic demand).

Concrete case I'm trying to explain:
- Morning: 5 min of light in-place jogging at chosen tempo in a bounded time window → breathing ample, no perceived breathlessness, high subjective comfort.
- Same day, ~6h later: brisk walk out of a grocery store while feeling "in a hurry" (self-imposed time pressure, not real deadline) → immediate perceived shortness of breath despite objectively lower effort than the morning jog.

The differential I want to test: it's not the effort itself that costs, it's the **imposed vs. self-chosen pace** and the associated shift in autonomic/postural/respiratory pattern.

## Hypotheses to evaluate

1. **Functional dyspnea / dysfunctional breathing pattern** is a well-recognized clinical entity (non-pathological in mild forms) with established mechanisms: thoracic-dominant shallow breathing, diaphragm-abdominal co-contraction, micro-apneas under stress.

2. **Self-paced vs. externally-paced (or self-hurried) effort** produces measurable differences in cardiorespiratory economy at matched external workload — perceived exertion, HR, VE/VO₂, breathing frequency.

3. **Emotional urgency / hurry** (even in absence of real time pressure) triggers a sympathetic shift that raises cardiac output and disturbs breathing pattern beyond what the metabolic demand requires.

4. **Postural chain engagement** (upright, aligned, weight-bearing through feet-hip-diaphragm) is a permissive condition for ample diaphragmatic breathing; distracted/loaded/hurried posture defeats this permissive condition.

5. **Respiration acts as the primary somatic pathway** through which imposed psychic pace is paid — because it's the voluntary/involuntary hinge and the first system to break under mismatch.

## Literature angles to cover

- **Dysfunctional breathing / breathing pattern disorder**: Nijmegen questionnaire, Courtney's classification (biomechanical / biochemical / psychophysiological), prevalence in general population, differential diagnosis vs. cardiac/pulmonary dyspnea.
- **Effort syndrome / Da Costa's syndrome / hyperventilation syndrome**: historical framing, contemporary reappraisal.
- **Self-paced vs. imposed-pace exercise**: pacing strategy literature, comparative economy studies, RPE differentials at matched workload (Foster, Tucker, Marcora, Noakes lineage).
- **Time pressure / hurry sickness and physiology**: cardiovascular reactivity to time-urgency, Type A behavior classic literature, contemporary work on perceived time pressure and autonomic activation.
- **Interoception and dyspnea perception**: Feldman Barrett, Craig, Paulus — predictive coding models of breathlessness, why perception can diverge from actual demand.
- **Diaphragm mechanics under stress**: co-contraction with abdominal wall, intra-abdominal pressure, thoracic vs. diaphragmatic breathing patterns.
- **Postural chain and respiration**: Hodges lineage on transversus abdominis / diaphragm coupling, upright posture and vital capacity, "postural-respiratory competition."
- **Autonomic pacing**: HRV, vagal tone at submaximal effort, self-determined vs. constrained-condition physiology.

## Report format requested

For each hypothesis (1–5):
- **Level of evidence**: strong / moderate / weak / speculative
- **Key references** (with DOI when possible, distinguishing peer-reviewed from other)
- **Contradicting evidence or important nuances**
- **Named concept / clinical entity** if the literature has one

Then:
- **Synthesis**: is the experiential distinction I describe well-supported, partially supported, or novel/underexplored?
- **Terminological guidance**: what vocabulary should I use in a public-facing article to be scientifically accurate without medicalizing an ordinary everyday phenomenon?
- **Research gaps**: what would be worth investigating empirically?

## Methodological notes

- Prioritize peer-reviewed physiology, respiratory medicine, sports science, psychophysiology.
- Distinguish clearly what is established vs. what is plausible extrapolation.
- Flag when a concept has clinical resonance but no clean operationalization (e.g., "hurry sickness" as folk term vs. as measurable construct).
- Include French-language sources if the phenomenon has a distinct French clinical tradition (dyspnée fonctionnelle, syndrome d'hyperventilation).
