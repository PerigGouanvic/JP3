# Is there research on selectively activating the soleus while standing, with minimal quadriceps and gastrocnemius activation — a standing analogue of Marc Hamilton's seated soleus push-up? I'm looking for EMG studies of standing heel raises, quiet-stance sway or low-amplitude bouncing comparing soleus vs vastus lateralis vs gastrocnemius activity, the effect of knee angle (near-extended vs flexed), and whether skeletal "stacking" reduces thigh muscle demand. Cite authors, year, journal.

Which brings me to : How did Marc Hamilton's lab teach the soleus push-up (SPU)? Is there evidence that the SPU is a motor skill with a learning curve: number of training sessions, EMG biofeedback, verbal cues about foot pressure and range of motion? Compare replications with vs without EMG feedback (e.g. Kraemer 2024 bioRxiv, Yuwono 2025 Sports MDPI) and any study on retention of correct SPU form without feedback.

And finally to:
How does plantar pressure location (first metatarsal head / medial forefoot vs lateral forefoot vs heel) influence soleus vs gastrocnemius recruitment during standing plantarflexion or heel raises? Include evidence on foot-sole cutaneous mechanoreceptors, proprioceptive training for selective triceps surae activation, and barefoot vs shod differences.

**Yes, there is relevant research, but it supports a distinction between “soleus-dominant standing activity” and a validated standing equivalent of Hamilton’s SPU.** Standing posture can produce relatively sustained soleus activity with little thigh activity, but knee flexion, gastrocnemius recruitment, movement amplitude, and metabolic effectiveness are separate questions that cannot be resolved from one EMG comparison.[^1][^2][^3]

**The clearest evidence on learning and feedback comes from Elek et al. (2025), not Yuwono:** both groups learned with EMG feedback, then one group performed the intervention without feedback the following day. That is evidence of short-term transfer after feedback-assisted instruction, not evidence that instruction or feedback is unnecessary.

*Scope note:* Some important primary-method details remain unverified below, particularly Hamilton’s original number of familiarization sessions, the proposed Kraemer attribution, and direct experiments manipulating first- versus fifth-metatarsal pressure. Those gaps should not be filled with generic calf-raise instructions.

## Standing soleus selectivity

### Posture matters independently

One particularly relevant study is **Hideaki Muraoka, Toshiaki Suzuki, and colleagues, 2021, *Journal of Physical Therapy Science*, 33:472–479**, “Effects of trunk anterior tilt and knee joint flexion angle changes on muscle activity in the lower limb muscles.” Twenty-eight healthy men performed nine standing postures while investigators recorded, among other muscles, soleus, medial gastrocnemius, and vastus lateralis.

Their results separate the variables unusually well: **vastus lateralis activity was affected by knee angle; soleus activity by trunk anterior tilt; medial gastrocnemius activity by both.** The EMG normalization reference was an isometric grade-3 manual muscle test, not a maximum voluntary contraction, so its percentages must not be interpreted as %MVC.

This is directly relevant to your hypothesis: an alignment that minimizes quadriceps demand does not necessarily minimize soleus activity, and changing knee angle does not necessarily affect soleus and gastrocnemius identically. However, this was a static-posture experiment, not a demonstration of a standing SPU or its metabolic effects.


| Evidence | Finding relevant to your question | Important qualification |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Muraoka, Suzuki et al., 2021, *Journal of Physical Therapy Science*** | In standing, VL depended on knee angle, SOL on trunk tilt, and MG on both. | Static postures; normalization was not MVC. |
| **Alrowayeh, Sabbahi \& Etnyre, 2005, *Journal of Neuroscience Methods*, 144:215–225** | Standing knee flexion increased vastus medialis EMG, especially at 60° compared with extension.[^3] | VM, not VL; voluntary EMG subsample only four people.[^3] |
| **Tikkanen et al., 2013, *PLOS ONE*, “Muscle Activity and Inactivity Periods during Normal Daily Life”** | Standing provided a low-activity reference for thigh-muscle recordings.[^2] | The approximately 2.5% MVC reference concerned combined thigh-muscle channels, not a soleus-to-VL ratio.[^2] |
| **Fok and colleagues, 2020, “Cosine tuning determines plantarflexors’ activities…”** | SOL showed more tonic activity and MG more phasic activity during quiet standing.[^1] | The retrieved material did not provide sufficiently complete bibliographic metadata to verify the journal here.[^1] |

### Quiet stance is already differentiated

Fok and colleagues describe the basic observation explicitly:

> “Soleus muscle shows a tonic activity whereas medial gastrocnemius muscle shows a phasic activity during quiet standing.”[^1]

Their research program asks how the nervous system distributes activity between plantarflexors that have different mechanical actions: soleus acts at the ankle, whereas gastrocnemius acts at both ankle and knee. Their analysis related recruitment to combinations of ankle and knee torque, rather than treating plantarflexion torque alone as sufficient to predict activity.[^1]

Thus, quiet stance can be soleus-dominant **in its temporal recruitment pattern**, without requiring deep knee flexion. That does not establish that quiet stance supplies the sustained oxidative demand achieved during the seated SPU.[^1]

### Does skeletal stacking help?

**“Stacking” is a reasonable mechanical description, but not a standardized intervention in these studies.** The closest operational question is whether the posture reduces the external moment that thigh muscles must resist; the standing knee-angle evidence supports lower quadriceps demand near extension than in a substantial crouch.[^3]

Alrowayeh, Sabbahi, and Etnyre measured the following **vastus medialis RMS EMG**, in microvolts, during standing:[^3]


| Knee flexion | VM RMS EMG, mean ± SD |
| :-- | --: |
| 0° | 11.25 ± 7.2 µV.[^3] |
| 30° | 24.96 ± 11.6 µV.[^3] |
| 45° | 32.47 ± 11.8 µV.[^3] |
| 60° | 55.99 ± 24.4 µV.[^3] |

The overall knee-angle effect was significant, but only the 0° versus 60° pairwise comparison reached significance in this four-person EMG subsample. These data support avoiding a deep crouch when the objective is low thigh-muscle demand, but they do not identify an optimal slight-flexion angle or justify deliberate knee hyperextension.[^3]

A further methodological point matters: most of that paper concerns **H-reflexes**, not ongoing voluntary activation. Its soleus H-reflex findings must not be read as direct measurements of how much soleus force or metabolic work each posture required.[^3]

### Bent knees do not guarantee isolation

The common instruction “bend the knees to isolate soleus” is too categorical for weight-bearing exercise. **Kim Hébert-Losier and Hans-Christer Holmberg, 2014, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 28:843–855**, noted earlier heel-raise experiments reporting no marked soleus or gastrocnemius surface-EMG difference between 0° and 45° knee flexion.

Their own experiment involved repeated maximal plantarflexions on a dynamometer, not standing heel raises, so that observation is a secondary citation rather than a direct replication. Nevertheless, it is an important warning against assuming that the seated bent-knee rationale transfers unchanged to standing.

The resulting trade-off is central to your question: **knee flexion may change gastrocnemius mechanics while simultaneously increasing quadriceps demand.** A posture favorable to relative soleus contribution is therefore not automatically favorable to minimal whole-leg recruitment.[^3]

For low-amplitude bouncing specifically, the sources verified here do **not** establish a protocol simultaneously producing elevated SOL, minimal MG/LG, and minimal VL. Nor do they establish metabolic equivalence between such bouncing and Hamilton’s seated SPU.

## How SPU was taught

### Hamilton’s original study

The original paper is **Marc T. Hamilton, Deborah G. Hamilton \& Theodore W. Zderic, 2022, *iScience*, 25(9):104869**, “A potent physiological method to magnify and sustain soleus oxidative metabolism improves glucose and lipid regulation.”

A later replication describes Hamilton’s experiments as controlled, prolonged soleus-contraction protocols with different activity intensities, rather than simply an instruction to perform arbitrary seated calf raises. Its account includes 1.3- and 1.7-MET conditions during glucose testing and a separate approximately 2-MET prolonged-contraction experiment.

However, the original teaching methods were not available in sufficient detail in the verified material to establish **the number or duration of familiarization sessions, a learning-to-criterion rule, exact foot-pressure cues, or a formal retention test**. It would therefore be unjustified to attribute a particular first-metatarsal cue or multi-session learning schedule to Hamilton’s lab.

### The documented feedback experiment

The fully documented study is **Dávid Elek, Miklós Tóth, Balázs Sonkodi, Pongrác Ács, Gábor L. Kovács, Péter Tardi \& Csaba Melczer, 2025, *Sports*, 13(3):81**, “The Efficacy of Soleus Push-Up in Individuals with Prediabetes: A Pilot Study,” DOI **10.3390/sports13030081**.

Its teaching procedure is unusually useful because it distinguishes **feedback during acquisition** from **feedback during subsequent performance**:


| Teaching component | What Elek et al. actually reported |
| :-- | :-- |
| Instrumentation | A wireless surface-EMG sensor over soleus, positioned according to SENIAM recommendations. |
| Initial reference task | Several minutes of comfortable treadmill walking while participants watched their soleus EMG. |
| Seated positioning | Knees bent, with the metatarsophalangeal joint line positioned below the knee; the stated purpose was to reduce gastrocnemius involvement. |
| Movement | Plantarflexion through an approximate 0–45° range, at about 60 repetitions/minute. |
| Performance target | Maintain markedly greater soleus activity than during the treadmill-walking reference. |
| Acquisition | All participants were reported to learn the movement in one session; session duration was not specified. |
| Subsequent test | A two-hour OGTT with SPUs, performed the following day, with or without ongoing EMG feedback. |

The authors write:

> “All participants successfully learned the movement within a single session…”

That statement describes their acquisition outcome, but the paper does not supply a trial-by-trial learning curve or a quantitative, prospectively defined “correct form” threshold.

Crucially, they monitored **soleus**, not a simultaneous soleus–gastrocnemius–quadriceps panel. Consequently, successful performance meant achieving their soleus-activity target; it did not demonstrate a specified suppression of gastrocnemius or thigh EMG.

### Feedback versus no feedback

Both groups received EMG-assisted teaching. During the next-day intervention, five participants continued with EMG feedback and five performed without it, using their previous practice to estimate intensity.


| Outcome | Continued feedback | No feedback during testing |
| :-- | --: | --: |
| Prior EMG-assisted instruction | Yes. | Yes. |
| Glucose iAUC reduction versus each group’s sedentary condition | Approximately 37%. | Approximately 26%. |
| Feedback during the intervention | Continuous visual soleus EMG. | None. |
| What this tests | Feedback-supported execution after instruction. | Next-day execution after feedback-assisted instruction. |

The between-group difference was not statistically significant, but this was a very small pilot with missing glucose observations. **Failure to detect a difference is not an equivalence demonstration**, particularly with approximately five participants per group.

The useful positive result is narrower: a glucose-lowering effect remained when continuous feedback was removed after instruction. It does not establish that novices can obtain the same result without ever receiving feedback.

### Learning and retention

The evidence therefore supports three different answers:

**SPU involves a learnable performance target:** yes, in the practical sense that participants were taught to alter and sustain soleus activation using a physiological reference and visual feedback.

**A measured learning curve:** not established by this study; it does not compare acquisition across repeated sessions or randomize participants to feedback-assisted versus feedback-free learning.

**Retention of objectively correct muscle selectivity:** not established; the no-feedback group’s next-day glucose response is evidence of short-term functional transfer, but not a retained SOL:MG:VL EMG ratio, because those simultaneous measurements were not reported.

The **“Yuwono 2025 Sports” attribution does not match the verified paper**, whose first author is Elek. The proposed “Kraemer 2024 bioRxiv” attribution could not be verified sufficiently to make a reliable study-by-study comparison; it should not be treated as a separate confirmed replication without its title or DOI.

## Pressure location and recruitment

### A medial-forefoot cue remains unproven

**The verified evidence does not establish “pressure under the first metatarsal head selectively recruits soleus rather than gastrocnemius.”** The most informative experiments concern regional sensory stimulation, which is not the same intervention as voluntarily shifting weight during a heel raise.[^4][^5]

This distinction separates three mechanisms: changing the mechanical loading configuration, changing cutaneous afferent input, and changing the participant’s voluntary coordination strategy. A pressure cue could affect all three, so a resulting EMG change would not automatically identify a soleus-specific sensory pathway.[^4][^5][^6]

A useful adjacent finding comes from **“Peroneal muscle activity during different types of walking,” 2018**: soleus activation was comparatively invariant across the tested level and medially inclined walking conditions, whereas the incline manipulations particularly concerned peroneal recruitment. That is not a heel-raise experiment, but it argues against assuming that changing medial–lateral foot loading necessarily changes soleus activation substantially.[^7]

### Heel and forefoot sensory input

A **2022 location-specific cutaneous-stimulation experiment** recorded soleus, medial gastrocnemius, and tibialis anterior while participants stood upright. Heel stimulation facilitated motor-evoked and cervicomedullary-evoked responses in **both soleus and medial gastrocnemius**; metatarsal stimulation did not significantly change those particular evoked responses.[^4]

That paper also discusses **Nakajima et al. (2006)**, who found facilitation of plantarflexor activity after heel stimulation and inhibition after forefoot stimulation. The relevant point is that the described effects involved **both plantarflexors**, not a demonstrated soleus-only channel.[^4]


| Regional input | Verified observation | What cannot be concluded |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Electrical heel stimulation | Facilitated evoked responses in SOL and MG in the 2022 experiment.[^4] | That consciously pressing the heel preferentially activates SOL.[^4] |
| Electrical metatarsal stimulation | Did not change the tested SOL/MG MEPs or CMEPs in that experiment.[^4] | That medial-forefoot loading suppresses MG while sparing SOL.[^4] |
| Forefoot versus heel vibration | Earlier work produced location-dependent sway responses.[^5] | That either location yields a metabolically effective standing SPU.[^5] |
| Medial-forefoot noisy vibration | Demonstrated cutaneomotor coupling in a 2020 experiment.[^5] | A learned, selectively retained increase in SOL relative to MG.[^5] |

The authors and journal metadata for those particular 2020 and 2022 papers were not fully exposed in the retrieved material, so they are identified by linked study rather than supplied with guessed bibliographic details.

### Mechanoreceptors are relevant, not exclusive

The **2020 noisy-vibration study** explains two roles of plantar cutaneous feedback: informing the nervous system about events at the foot–ground interface and triggering stabilizing muscle responses. Its experimental stimulation site was the medial forefoot near the base of the first toe, and the measured response delays were consistent with polysynaptic cutaneomotor pathways.[^5]

That establishes a physiologically meaningful sensory route from the medial forefoot to lower-limb motor output. It does **not** establish that the route preferentially excites soleus over gastrocnemius, or that sustained voluntary pressure reproduces an electrical or vibratory stimulus.[^5][^4]

The relationship also should not be simplified to “more sensation equals more soleus activation”: a **2023 study of reduced foot-sole sensitivity** discusses similar modulation of soleus and gastrocnemius H-reflexes and their synergistic postural role.[^8]

## Proprioceptive training and footwear

### Selective training needs selective measurement

The documented SPU teaching experiment provides evidence for learning to increase soleus EMG relative to a walking reference. It does not establish learning to dissociate soleus from gastrocnemius, because increasing one recorded muscle is not equivalent to proving that its synergists remain quiet.

For your specific question, the decisive training experiment would need simultaneous normalized recordings from SOL, MG, LG, and VL, together with pressure mapping and controlled joint angles. This requirement follows from the existing evidence that posture affects these muscles differently and that plantar sensory stimulation can influence SOL and MG together.[^4]

A delayed test without displayed feedback would then distinguish immediate performance assistance from retained coordination. Elek et al. provide a useful next-day functional test, but not that full muscle-selectivity retention test.

### Barefoot versus shod

There is good evidence in the retrieved work that plantar sensory input contributes to balance control. **That is not sufficient evidence that barefoot heel raises selectively increase SOL relative to MG or VL.** None of the verified experiments here supplies the direct barefoot-versus-shod comparison needed for that conclusion.[^5][^4]

Likewise, footwear effects on balance or sensory input should not be substituted for evidence about SPU technique, soleus selectivity, or glucose regulation. These remain separate outcomes requiring direct measurement.[^5]

## The unresolved experimental question

The most defensible interpretation is that **near-extended standing offers a plausible way to keep thigh demand low, while quiet stance already demonstrates differentiated soleus and gastrocnemius recruitment.** What remains unestablished is whether a deliberately trained, small-amplitude standing movement can substantially elevate soleus activity without proportionally recruiting gastrocnemius, and then reproduce the seated SPU’s metabolic effects.[^3][^1]

A discriminating experiment would compare near-extension, slight flexion, and a deeper bent-knee condition during quiet sway and small heel raises, while independently controlling medial versus lateral forefoot loading. It would need to report **absolute normalized SOL activity as well as SOL:MG and SOL:VL relationships**, because an improved ratio alone could result from lowering the other muscle rather than meaningfully increasing soleus work.[^4]

For the teaching question, the missing comparison is equally specific: **feedback-assisted acquisition versus genuinely feedback-free acquisition**, followed by delayed, undisplayed EMG verification. The published Elek pilot answers the narrower and encouraging question of whether continuous feedback can be removed the next day after an EMG-assisted lesson.
