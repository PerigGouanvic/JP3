## Tour 1 — Nature du réflexe quand le talon frappe le sol avec force

**Question** : What is the nature of the reflex without occurs when you are running or walking fast and you're hitting the ground with your heel and it seems that if the contact is strong the foot naturally and reflexively pushes back without any effort of the habitual nature of running

The best-supported explanation is that you are feeling two things at once: a spinal stretch reflex layered on passive elastic recoil. Together they are the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC). I found no study that isolates this for a heel strike specifically, so the heel-strike part below is my inference.

### The stretch reflex

When the foot lands, the calf muscle-tendon unit (the soleus above all) is suddenly stretched. Muscle spindles detect the stretch, and the signal goes to the spinal cord and back to the same muscle, which contracts without any cortical command. A teaching document on the SSC describes the circuit like this: "the muscle spindle receptors send an action potential, through a sensory neuron, to the integrating centre within the spinal cord which then sends the action potential, through a motor neuron, to cause the muscle being stretched to contract."

Paavo Komi's group in Jyväskylä measured this in running and hopping. His review in the Journal of Biomechanics (2000) reports "a very pronounced contribution of the short-latency stretch-reflex component" to force generation at the stretch-to-shortening transition in running and hopping. That component "appears about 40 ms after the ground contact" and is "especially strong in the soleus muscle". Contact lasts roughly 90 to 100 ms in maximal sprinting, so the reflex has time to act within a single stance.

### Elastic recoil

The stiffening of the muscle-tendon complex under a fast stretch also stores energy, which is released in the push-off. The same teaching text notes that the SSC "begins with an eccentric contraction", which makes the complex stiffer, and that the stored elastic energy is returned in the next concentric contraction. So part of what feels like a reflexive push-back is the tendon springing back, which needs no neural command at all.

### Why a harder strike pushes harder

- The reflex depends on stretch speed and amplitude. Komi's review says that once the "critical" tension is reached, the forceful stretch is determined by the amount of preactivation sent to the muscles before contact.
- Your brain therefore sets the gain before landing, since you anticipate the impact. The reflex and the tendon then scale the response to the strike.
- Timing matters. The teaching text warns that if the transition from stretch to shortening is slow, the stored energy "may be lost as heat". A quick, firm contact keeps it.
- Tendon properties vary between athletes and measurement methods, and the evidence on sprinters versus distance runners is mixed. I would not read a fixed stiffness target into any of this.

### Heel strike caveat

The soleus reflex data come mostly from hopping and forefoot or midfoot contact. With a true heel strike, the ankle is dorsiflexed and the calf is not loaded in the same way at first. The push-back you feel may then come from a few sources:

- The calf stretch as the foot rolls down onto the ground.
- Knee and hip extensors, which are also loaded by the impact.
- A general extension reflex of the stance limb. This is often called the extensor thrust or positive supporting reaction, and the crossed-extensor reflex is a related spinal reflex that supports the body on the opposite limb.

I could not verify which of these dominates in heel-striking, so treat that as a hypothesis. To test it, record soleus, gastrocnemius and quadriceps EMG during heel-strike and forefoot-strike running at the same speed, and compare the onset latencies with Komi's roughly 40 ms.

## Tour 2 — Pogo vs. wheel : extensor thrust et propulsion forward vs. upwards

**Question** : the extensor thrust or positive supporting reaction are two terms that resonate with experience because I feel a thrust forward when giving more weight to heel contact, as if the whole geometry of the foot-leg complex was geared towards walking forward, but especially if you properly land with the heel. Then it's all the back chain that activates, and there's a spontaneous, or should I say necessary propulsion forward. In contrast, overdoing forefoot or midfoot contact in a attempt to gain more propulsive strength amounts to upwards propulsion. To use an image, one is like Pogo jumping, the other is like a wheel, or circular spring

Your two images match two models in the biomechanics literature: a spring-mass "pogo" with a point foot, and the same spring with a rolling "wheel" foot. I found no study that tests your specific claim about heel contact and a thrust forward, so the link to extensor thrust is my interpretation.

### The wheel image has a literature

Clinical gait analysis describes the foot as a rolling wheel: "the ankle-foot complex can be likened to the rolling of a wheel as the center of pressure (COP) moves from heel to toe". It splits stance into three rockers:

- The heel rocker runs from initial contact until the forefoot touches down.
- The ankle rocker follows, with dorsiflexion while the foot is flat.
- The forefoot rocker begins when the heel lifts and ends at toe-off.

Together they make the "roll-over shape", the curved path the COP follows relative to the lower leg.

The pogo has an equivalent too. Whittington and Thelen's spring-mass model of walking notes that "the classic spring-mass model has a point foot and therefore a fixed COP during stance." Adding circular roller feet (radius about 0.3 m) "allowed for forward center-of-pressure progression", and reduced peak ground reaction forces. Their conclusion: "a simple mass-spring model with roller feet can well describe ground reaction forces, and hence center of mass motion, observed during normal human walking." That is close to your "circular spring". The roller also shortens "the horizontal distance between the COP and COM".

### Why a point contact pushes you up

I infer this from the model, not from a study of forefoot running. A point foot under the body keeps the ground reaction force close to vertical, so a stronger push mostly lifts the centre of mass. A contact that starts behind the centre of mass and rolls forward moves the force's line of action across the body and keeps it directed forward. That fits your feeling that overdone forefoot contact gives "upwards propulsion".

### The costs of the heel collision

Heel contact is not free propulsion, and the walking literature says so plainly. Kuo, Donelan and Ruina state that inverted-pendulum walking "unavoidably requires mechanical work to redirect the body's center of mass in the transition between steps", and that this work is a "major determinant of the overall cost of walking". The leading leg's heel strike is dissipative: "the leading leg performs negative work on the COM", so positive work must restore the lost energy. The trailing leg's push-off before heel strike is the economical remedy, because it "reduces the collision velocity and, therefore, the amount of work that must be performed".

So in walking, the forward drive you feel after heel contact probably comes from two sources. One is the pendulum arc carrying you over the stance leg. The other is the trailing leg's push-off, with the posterior chain (calf, hamstrings, glutes) working eccentrically then concentrically. I could not verify that a reflex adds to this, so the extensor-thrust link stays a hypothesis.

### Running differs from walking

The studies above concern walking. Running with a heel strike is a bouncing gait, and the spring-mass model is its standard description. Your wheel image fits the roll-through of the foot. The vertical impact transient and the braking at heel strike are real costs that I did not research here.

### A test you could run

Compare the same speed with heel-first and forefoot-first contact, and track the horizontal and vertical ground reaction force components, the COP path, and soleus and hamstring EMG. If your account holds, heel-first contact should show a longer COP path and a smaller vertical-to-horizontal impulse ratio.

## Tour 3 — DIY measurement methods

**Question** : Yeah, do you where I could find a couple of million dollars for the measuring equipment? I barely can afford food

No millions needed. Your question is mostly about timing, centre-of-pressure path and force direction. A hundred dollars of sensors and some free software can get you a long way on those.

### Cheapest first

- **Slow-motion video plus Kinovea.** Kinovea is free software for frame-by-frame analysis, and studies use it to measure joint angles, stride length and phase durations from running videos. A phone filming at 240 fps is enough to compare heel-first and forefoot-first contact. It will show foot angle at contact, contact time and where the foot sits relative to the body. It gives no forces.
- **DIY load-cell plate.** A University of Wisconsin design project built a low-cost force plate with a budget of \$100 to \$500. A commercial 2D plate from BioRow costs over \$2000, and small multi-axis load cells run \$300 to \$500. Cheaper single-axis load cells with an amplifier would give vertical force only. That is enough for the vertical-impulse side of your test, but not the forward component.
- **Pressure insoles.** Moticon insoles are "wireless, easy to use and they have suitable prices", though they "systemically underestimate forces" compared with the PedarX gold standard. They fit your COP-path question, because they show where the pressure moves from heel to toe.

### Estimating forces with IMUs

Wearable inertial sensors can estimate ground reaction forces without a plate. One study on rearfoot-strike runners used IMUs on the wrist and sternum with a neural network. It reached an error of 0.10 ± 0.024 body weight against force-plate data, and the outdoor results held up. Another used three IMUs on the pelvis and both tibiae and got an RMSE of about 0.13 body weight for vertical force. A third estimated anterior-posterior as well as vertical GRF in continuous running from IMUs, which is the forward component you care about.

These are research methods that need trained models, and the studies were done on rearfoot strikers. I would not trust them to distinguish a subtle difference between two landing styles. The estimates are also less accurate than lab systems.

### Other routes

- **Borrow a lab.** Kistler's new portable plate (9260BA) is aimed at gait work and can be chained up to 16 plates into a walkway. A university kinesiology or physiotherapy department with a plate may let you run a small pilot, especially if you propose a clean protocol. That is cheaper than buying anything.
- **OpenSim.** This free musculoskeletal modelling software can take ground reaction force data.

I would start with Kinovea and a phone, since it costs nothing and may already show the difference in foot geometry and contact. Add a DIY load cell or insoles only if the video leaves a question open. You may want to try the video test first and tell me what you see.

## Tour 4 — Évolution du pied humain : Holowka-Lieberman, Welte, Laetoli, windlass

**Question** : On the subjective side, I am satisfied. I've been doing mostly stationary jogging at home, with wonderful effects on my alertness and cardio. However, the back chain dominance gained by affording a few more milliseconds to the heel causes an immediate propulsion forward, which is unmistakable. It's really as if I had discovered what people with natural back chain dominance, who are often extremely athletic, do naturally. One paradox is that this posture is much more upright, but still much more propulsive at a 90 angle of this, forward. As opposed to the front foot dominance, that feels like running from starting blocks, but that do not afford the same level of forward propulsion. The muscle/bone/ligaments/fasciae apparatus has acquired a directionality that is unmatched in the animal kingdom, because the foot is what makes us human

Your description fits what the foot research says about heel-first contact: a foot that is compliant at landing and stiff at push-off. The research also suggests a refinement to the "unmatched in the animal kingdom" claim.

### A foot built for heel-to-toe

Holowka and Lieberman (2018, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) reviewed the experimental work on the biomechanics of "foot strike, push-off and elastic energy storage in the foot". They found that "several unique features of the human foot, including a spring-like longitudinal arch and short toes, are likely adaptations to long distance running." Welte and colleagues put the arch's role this way: it "is unique among hominins as it is compliant at ground contact but sufficiently stiff to enable push-off". That is the geometry you describe: the heel lands on a compliant structure, and the foot becomes a lever as the load rolls forward.

The Laetoli footprints, about 3.6 million years old, already show this pattern. Analysis of the footprints indicated "a pronounced heel strike from deep impressions", a "well-developed medial longitudinal arch", and a deep big-toe impression "commensurate with toe-off". The heel-first roll-through is therefore very old in our lineage.

### Where the claim needs care

- **Not unique in every respect.** Holowka and Lieberman found that "humans and great apes share some underappreciated, surprising similarities in foot function, such as use of plantigrady and ability to stiffen the midfoot." What looks specific to humans is the combination: the arch spring, short toes and a stiff push-off lever.
- **The windlass is contested.** The textbook story is that Hicks' windlass stiffens the foot at push-off. A Yale dissertation on foot stiffness argues otherwise: the transverse arch contributes more to stiffness, and the windlass "is not a foot stiffener but a power transmission mechanism" that lets the midfoot output power "by tapping into the output of muscles outside the foot". A 2024 simulation paper calls it "unclear whether the windlass mechanism contributes to stiffening the foot". The same paper found that a stiff plantar fascia, sufficient arch height and the intrinsic foot muscles all mattered for high ankle push-off power.
- **Stationary jogging.** In place, there is no net horizontal impulse over a cycle. What you feel as a forward drive is probably the orientation of the force and the readiness of the posterior chain, which fits your point about upright posture with a forward direction of propulsion. If you want to separate that from real propulsion, jog slowly forward and compare.

I could not verify your claim that natural posterior-chain-dominant athletes move this way. It is plausible and consistent with the mechanics above, but I did not find a study on it.

## Points pivots pour la doctrine JP³

1. **"In place, there is no net horizontal impulse over a cycle"** — Perplexity clarifie : la sensation de propulsion avant en JP³ = *orientation de la force + readiness de la chaîne postérieure*, pas translation réelle. Raffine la grammaire de re-pondération des 3 P (★★ 2026-08-27) : activation directionnelle ≠ débordement In Place strict.
2. **Welte — "compliant at ground contact but sufficiently stiff to enable push-off"** — formulation canonique de la géométrie fonctionnelle du pied humain, directement lisible comme description du JP³ en régime talon-activateur.
3. **Laetoli 3.6 Mya — heel-strike archaic pattern** — le contact talonnier est un héritage très ancien (Australopithèque), ancre le talon-activateur dans une profondeur évolutive qui désamorce le discours "le heel-strike c'est mauvais" répandu dans les milieux running moderne.
4. **Kuo-Donelan-Ruina — collision economy** — le heel strike n'est pas "free propulsion" mais entre dans un bilan économique où le push-off de la jambe arrière compense la dissipation. En JP³ In Place, le bilan boucle sur place (pas de translation), mais les deux phases se produisent.
5. **Extensor thrust / positive supporting reaction / crossed-extensor reflex** — hypothèse spinale pour la sensation de poussée arrière automatique au contact talon. Non isolée pour heel strike spécifiquement — à tester en EMG.
