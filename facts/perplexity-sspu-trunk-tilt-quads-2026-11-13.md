# Perplexity — trunk tilt & quadriceps (follow-up SSPU) — 2026-11-13



I'd like you to explain how exactly the tilt of the trunk can minimize the involvement of unwanted muscles such as the quads

Trunk tilt doesn't remove quadriceps demand directly. It moves the ground reaction force line in front of the knee, so gravity extends the knee passively. The quadriceps then have less to do, but the load moves to the soleus, gastrocnemius and hip extensors. The mechanism below is my own biomechanical reasoning, anchored to the sources cited. No study I found tests it for a standing soleus push-up.

The mechanism

The quadriceps work to resist an external knee flexion moment. That moment equals the vertical ground reaction force multiplied by the horizontal distance between the knee joint centre and the ground reaction force line. A source on gait biomechanics summarizes it this way: "KFM is the external moment that tends to flex the knee during the stance phase and it is primarily counteracted by the quadriceps."[1]

In quiet standing the line of force passes close to the centre of mass. The line therefore falls behind the knee in a crouch, and the quadriceps must hold the knee up. If the line falls in front of the knee, the moment extends the knee and the quadriceps are not needed.

Leaning the trunk forward moves the centre of mass, and with it the line, forward. A gait review describes the same trick as a compensation: "the trunk of the patient leans forward in order to shift the GRF in front of the knee, creating an extending moment." The head, arms and trunk make up about 55% of body mass, so a modest lean moves the line substantially.[2][3]

Why the soleus gets the load

The same forward shift increases the distance between the line of force and the ankle. That raises the plantarflexion moment the calf must produce. In lunge research, a forward trunk lean "significantly increase[d] the ankle plantar flexor moment, hip extensor moment, and activation of gluteus maximus and biceps femoris muscles relative to a vertical trunk posture." Muraoka and Suzuki's standing experiment (2021, Journal of Physical Therapy Science) fits this pattern. It found that "VL was affected by KA, and SOL was affected by TA", where KA is knee flexion angle and TA is trunk anterior tilt. In that study, knee angle moved the quadriceps and trunk tilt moved the soleus. Tilt did not change vastus lateralis activity.[3][4]

Caveats

Muraoka and Suzuki's results do not show that tilting the trunk lowers quadriceps activity. They show that vastus lateralis tracked knee angle, not tilt. The mechanism above predicts the benefit only if the knees stay near extension while the trunk tilts. If tilting makes you crouch, the quadriceps load rises.

Gastrocnemius is the main complication. A musculoskeletal model of standing found that "in forward-leaning postures, the medial gastrocnemius generated most of the required plantar flexion moment, followed by the soleus and lateral gastrocnemius." The gastrocnemii also supplied a knee flexion moment "to keep the knee from hyperextending." When the line of force is ahead of the knee, something has to stop the knee from locking backward. The gastrocnemius does that, so it is the muscle most likely to be recruited by this strategy. In the human data, MG was affected by both tilt and knee angle, as were the glutes and hamstrings.[4][5]

The Muraoka authors also saw no soleus change with knee flexion. They suggest this was because "the posterior tilt angle of the thigh and the anterior tilt angle of the lower leg" were similar, with only a minimal shift of the centre of gravity. This supports the idea that the soleus responds to where the centre of gravity sits over the foot, and not to knee angle alone.[6]

Practical implication for your design

The study supports a combined strategy: knees near extension, a small hip hinge so the load line sits just ahead of the knee, and recording MG alongside SOL and VL. Whether the gastrocnemius rises along with the soleus is the open question. Only simultaneous SOL, MG, LG and VL recordings at graded tilt angles would answer it. I did not find a published study with that design.
